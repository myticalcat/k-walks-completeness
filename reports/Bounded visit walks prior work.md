# 1990 k-walk papers anticipate most README claims

Most of what the README proves is already in the literature in closed-walk form, and the parts that are not are routine open-walk adaptations rather than new results. Closed walks that visit every vertex exactly k times, or between 1 and k times, were shown NP-complete for every fixed k by Broersma and Göbel (1990) and by Jackson and Wormald (1990, Theorem 6.1). For 1-connected graphs, the Jackson–Wormald construction is literally "attach k−1 pendant leaves to every vertex and reduce from HAMILTON CYCLE". The README's other gadget, K−1 pendant triangles for the exact version, appears in Liu, Sheffield and Westover (2024, Lemma 1), and both gadgets are solved homework problems at UIUC and MIT for k = 2. The edge-multiplicity lemma is explicit in Jackson–Wormald's definition of an exact k-walk. The open s–t form, with endpoint degrees 2r(v)−1, appears in the Many-Visits TSP literature. Jackson–Wormald also state the G[K̄_k] equivalence for Hamilton cycles. The research found no explicit statement of three things: the open-walk (HAMILTONIAN PATH) versions of claims (1) and (2), and the traceability half of the lexicographic remark. All three are easy corollaries, and their one non-routine step is handling walks that start or end inside a gadget. The README should therefore present its results as "open-walk variants of classical closed-walk results, with complete proofs and machine checks", not as new NP-completeness theorems. One caveat limits this verdict. No one on the research team read Broersma–Göbel's full text, and its preprint title, "k-traceable graphs", suggests it may already cover open walks.

## Closed-walk hardness and both gadgets date to 1990

The foundational result is H. J. Broersma and F. Göbel, "k-traversable graphs", *Ars Combinatoria* 29A (1990), pp. 141–153 ([zbMATH 0722.05045](https://zbmath.org/4189769)). It defines a graph as k-traversable if it has a closed walk in which every vertex occurs exactly k times. Liu, Sheffield and Westover summarise it this way: "for fixed k, is there a closed walk that visits each vertex exactly k times, or at least once and at most k times, respectively? They showed that for constant k, both of these problems are NP-hard" ([Liu, Sheffield, Westover 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16270)). Jackson and Wormald, writing the same year from Broersma's preprint, give a slightly different account. In their version, Broersma proved the exact case, and "with the proof given, G has a Hamilton cycle iff G′ has any k-walk, and thus the question of whether a given graph has a k-walk is NP-complete." They add that Broersma's reduction graphs "have many cut-vertices", which is consistent with pendant gadgets ([Jackson & Wormald 1990, §6](https://ajc-new.maths.uq.edu.au/pdf/2/ocr-ajc-v2-p135.pdf)).

Jackson and Wormald's own **Theorem 6.1** states: "For k and j fixed, K-WALK IN J-CONNECTED GRAPH and EXACT K-WALK IN J-CONNECTED GRAPH are NP-complete." The proof forms H = G[K_j] and joins jk−1 separate copies of K_j to each inflated vertex. For j = 1 this is H = G with **k−1 pendant leaves at every vertex**, reduced from HAMILTON CYCLE. That is exactly the README's ATMOST-K gadget in the closed setting ([Jackson & Wormald 1990, p. 145](https://ajc-new.maths.uq.edu.au/pdf/2/ocr-ajc-v2-p135.pdf)). The acknowledgements add that G. Pruesse's 1990 Toronto M.Sc. thesis, *A Generalization of Hamiltonicity*, independently obtained "a generalisation of Theorem 6.1". That thesis is not indexed online ([Math Genealogy](https://mathgenealogy.org/id.php?id=103665)).

The exact-visit gadget is just as well documented. Liu et al. define [a,b]-HAM as a closed walk visiting every vertex at least a and at most b times, and attribute their **Lemma 1** to Broersma–Göbel: [a,b]-HAM is NP-hard for all constants 1 ≤ a ≤ b. Its proof builds G′ "by gluing to each vertex b − 1 triangles" and reducing from Hamiltonian cycle ([Liu et al. 2024, §1.3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16270)). With a = b = K, this is the README's EXACT-K construction for closed walks, gadget for gadget. Liu et al. call such forced substructures "depletors". Their main theorems give exact P-versus-NP-hard dichotomies for d-regular and maximum-degree-d graphs. The secondary sources do not establish whether Broersma–Göbel themselves used triangles.

Jackson and Wormald write that "the proof for exact k-walks is exactly the same". That holds for j ≥ 2 but fails for their j = 1 case. In an exact k-walk, a pendant leaf needs degree 2k, so its one edge carries multiplicity 2k. That uses up its neighbour's entire degree budget and disconnects the walk whenever k ≥ 2 and G has at least two vertices. This is the obstruction the README explains under "Leaves fail here", and it is our own reading of the published proof, not a statement in any source. The practical consequence: the README should cite Jackson–Wormald for the leaf gadget in the at-most case, and Liu et al. (or the CS/ECE 374 solution) for the triangle gadget in the exact case.

The k = 2 cases are standard teaching material. Erickson's *Algorithms* (Ch. 12) poses the "double-Hamiltonian tour" (a closed walk visiting every vertex exactly twice) and the "triple-Hamiltonian tour" as exercises ([Erickson, Algorithms Ch. 12](https://jeffe.cs.illinois.edu/teaching/algorithms/book/12-nphard.pdf)). So do his 2010 graduate notes ([UIUC CS 573 Fa10](https://courses.grainger.illinois.edu/cs573/fa2010/notes/30-nphard.pdf)). UIUC CS/ECE 374 (Spring 2017, HW10) publishes a full solution that attaches a **pendant triangle** (v♭, v♯) to every vertex and reduces from Hamiltonian cycle. It also flags a "common incorrect solution (self-loops)" with a counterexample, the same pitfall the README documents in "Why a gadget is needed" ([CS/ECE 374 Sp17 HW10](https://courses.grainger.illinois.edu/cs374/sp2017/homework/hw10.pdf)). Demaine's MIT 6.890 (Fall 2014, PS3) proves the closed at-most-twice version with a triangle. It notes that "**many of the given solutions add a single leaf to every node. This is also correct**", and that the leaf reduction is parsimonious ([MIT 6.890 F14 PS3 solutions](https://courses.csail.mit.edu/6.890/fall14/psets/ps3-solutions.pdf)). Students rediscovering the leaf gadget independently is about as clear a sign of folklore status as one can find.

## The open versions are routine adaptations with one real wrinkle

None of the sources checked defines or analyses the open-walk problems. Every definition of a k-walk is a *closed* spanning walk: Jackson–Wormald, the WEGA wiki ([WEGA: K-Walk](https://pco.iis.nsk.su/wega/index.php/K-Walk)), Kaiser et al. ([Kaiser, Kužel, Li, Wang](http://home.zcu.cz/~kaisert/papers/deg-walk.pdf)) and Gao–Pasechnik ([arXiv:1412.0514](https://arxiv.org/html/1412.0514v4)). Liu et al. study closed walks only, and every course exercise found is closed. The "Hamiltonian walk" literature is a different problem: it minimises the length of a spanning walk rather than bounding visits ([Goodman & Hedetniemi 1974](https://epubs.siam.org/doi/10.1137/0203017); [Vacek 1991](https://eudml.org/doc/18320)).

The nearest open-walk relatives use the same reduction pattern on neighbouring problems. Abasi, Bshouty and Gabizon prove r-SIMPLE k-PATH NP-complete for every r, by reduction from HAMILTONIAN PATH. That problem asks for an open walk of a given length visiting each vertex at most r times, with no requirement to cover every vertex ([Abasi et al., arXiv:1312.4863](https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.4863)). Singh and Zenklusen show that "containing a k-trail" is NP-complete for every k ≥ 2, by attaching k−2 pendant leaves per vertex to a cubic HAMILTONIAN PATH instance ([Singh & Zenklusen, arXiv:1512.01781](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.01781)). Degree-constrained spanning tree, Garey–Johnson's classic, falls to the same "pendant fan from HAMILTONIAN PATH" move ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degree-constrained_spanning_tree); [CS/ECE 374 Sp17 Lab 14](https://courses.grainger.illinois.edu/cs374/sp2017/labs/solutions/lab14-sol.pdf)). Reducing from HAMILTONIAN PATH with pendant leaves is a stock technique, not an invention.

Swapping cycle for path changes exactly one thing in the proofs. A closed walk can never end inside a gadget, but an open walk can. The forward direction is unchanged. The converse must absorb endpoint effects. For ATMOST-K, an end of the walk at a leaf of v lets v repeat once more after projection. For EXACT-K, the endpoint deficits ε shift degree from the hub into a triangle, which produces the README's case table. The README handles both carefully, and that endpoint bookkeeping is its genuine, if modest, technical content. An independent re-derivation of the ATMOST-K converse during this review reached the same argument. The fair classification of claims (1) and (2) is therefore "**folklore-level corollaries, apparently unpublished in open form**", not "new results".

That "apparently" carries real weight. Jackson and Wormald cite Broersma's preprint as "**k-traceable graphs**" ([Jackson & Wormald 1990, references](https://ajc-new.maths.uq.edu.au/pdf/2/ocr-ajc-v2-p135.pdf)), and "traceable" conventionally means "has a Hamiltonian path". The published title became "k-traversable", and both secondary accounts describe only closed walks. It is still a live possibility that Broersma–Göbel treat the open variant. Later literature also uses "k-traceable" for an unrelated property: every induced subgraph of order k has a Hamiltonian path ([Electron. J. Combin. 18 (2011) P63](https://www.combinatorics.org/ojs/index.php/eljc/article/download/v18i1p63/pdf/)). Searches on that term will therefore return noise.

## The Euler lemma and binary-K membership are standard machinery

Claim (3) is explicitly published in four forms. Jackson and Wormald *define* an exact k-walk as "a connected spanning subgraph W of (2k)×G, such that the degree of each vertex v in W is 2k", and invoke Euler's theorem to equate it with a closed walk visiting every vertex exactly k times ([Jackson & Wormald 1990, p. 135](https://ajc-new.maths.uq.edu.au/pdf/2/ocr-ajc-v2-p135.pdf)). Liu et al. state the [a,b] form: "G is [a,b]-HAM if and only if 2b·G contains a spanning even [2a,2b] factor … The equivalence of this formulation follows from taking an Euler tour" ([Liu et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16270)). Berger, Kozma, Mnich and Vincze prove the directed closed version as Lemma 2.1, with infinite costs allowed ([arXiv:1804.06361](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06361)). Most directly relevant is Bérczi, Mnich and Vincze's open s–t version from the metric many-visits path TSP. There, z represents a feasible tour iff supp(z) is connected, "deg_z(v) = 2·r(v) holds for all v ∈ V − {s,t} and **deg_z(v) = 2·r(v) − 1 for v ∈ {s,t}**". That is the README's "two vertices of degree 2K−1" case ([Bérczi, Mnich, Vincze, EGRES TR-2020-19](https://egres.elte.hu/tr/egres-20-19.pdf)). Their setting is a complete graph with self-loops, where feasibility is never in question. The README's "one vertex of degree 2K−2" alternative is the s = t case, which is a routine merge of the trail and circuit forms of Euler's theorem.

NP membership with K in binary follows from the same representation, and the Many-Visits TSP community has treated it as standard for four decades. Cosmadakis and Papadimitriou's algorithm runs in time "exponential in the number of cities, but logarithmic in the number of visits", which is only possible because it outputs edge multiplicities rather than the walk itself ([Cosmadakis & Papadimitriou, MIT-LCS-TM-208](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/149019)). Kowalik et al. call this "MVTSP with compressed output, namely the output is a multiplicity function". With costs in {0, +∞}, they formalise the unweighted decision problem "Decision Unweighted Fixed Degree Connected Subgraph", which generalises directed Hamiltonian cycle, and solve it in randomized O*(2^n poly(M)) time ([Kowalik et al., arXiv:2005.02329](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.02329)). None of the passages checked phrases the result as an "∈ NP" theorem, so the README's membership remark is best labelled a standard observation. Closed EXACT-K on a graph is MV-TSP feasibility with forbidden edges set to ∞, so the README could also point readers to those exact exponential algorithms. In the papers reviewed, the k-walk papers do not cite the Many-Visits TSP literature, and the Many-Visits TSP papers do not cite Broersma–Göbel or Jackson–Wormald. That separation between two communities is the most plausible reason the result looks new from either side.

## The remarks restate known facts, and one understates them

**Lexicographic products (4a).** Jackson and Wormald's introduction states that "for k ≥ 2, G has a k-walk (or exact k-walk) if and only if G[K_k] (or G[K̄_k], respectively) has a Hamilton cycle". They use this connection explicitly "in examining the complexity of finding k-walks" ([Jackson & Wormald 1990, §1](https://ajc-new.maths.uq.edu.au/pdf/2/ocr-ajc-v2-p135.pdf)). The only available text is an OCR scan, which drops the complement bar, and two of the research notes transcribed the pairing in reverse. The mathematics forces the reading that matches the README: copies of v must be non-adjacent for each one to be a separate visit, so exact walks go with K̄_k. Anyone quoting the sentence should check it against the typeset original. Combined with Theorem 6.1, it makes HAMILTONIAN CYCLE NP-complete on graphs of the form G[K̄_k] as an immediate corollary. Specialising Liu et al.'s Theorem 2 to a = b = k keeps this true even when G has maximum degree 3 ([Liu et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16270)). No source states the HAMILTONIAN PATH (traceability) version. Ekstein and Teska fully characterise traceability of P_n[H], and remark that when G has no Hamiltonian path "it seems to be complicated to characterize when G[H] is traceable" ([Ekstein & Teska, DMGT 45 (2025), arXiv:2012.10123](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.10123)). Specialising their theorems to H = K̄_2 shows that P_3[K̄_2] is not traceable, which is exactly the README's a–b–c example. Kriesell's connected-2|H|-regular-multiple condition ([zbMATH 0883.68102](https://zbmath.org/1089088)) and Teichert's 1983 traceability conditions ([zbMATH 0535.05039](https://zbmath.org/?q=an:0535.05039)) are the natural product-graph citations.

**Closed walks (4b).** This remark is a restatement of Broersma–Göbel, Jackson–Wormald Theorem 6.1 and Liu et al. Lemma 1, and should be labelled as such. Its special handling of n ≤ 2, where K₂ yields a doubled edge, mirrors Jackson–Wormald's caveat that "for a graph with at least three vertices, every 1-walk is a Hamilton cycle".

**Large K (4c).** The bound K ≥ n−1 is correct but needlessly weak. Jackson–Wormald Lemma 2.2 shows that a spanning tree of maximum degree k yields a k-walk by doubling its edges, and they note that every connected graph has an α(G)-walk ([Jackson & Wormald 1990, pp. 137–138](https://ajc-new.maths.uq.edu.au/pdf/2/ocr-ajc-v2-p135.pdf)). Kaiser et al. observe that "a tree of maximum degree Δ has a Δ-walk … and it clearly does not admit any k-walk with k < Δ" ([Kaiser et al.](http://home.zcu.cz/~kaisert/papers/deg-walk.pdf)). Every spanning tree of G has maximum degree at most Δ(G), and the README's own DFS argument visits v exactly deg_T(v) times. **The README's proof therefore already shows that ATMOST-K is trivial once K ≥ Δ(G)**, and that is the threshold it should state. Liu et al.'s Theorem 2 shows this boundary is tight for closed walks: in graphs of maximum degree d, [1,b]-HAM is NP-hard iff b < d.

**Trails.** The README says the trail versions "are not covered here". The closed exact-K trail version is in fact classical. A spanning closed trail visiting every vertex exactly K times is an Euler circuit of a connected 2K-regular spanning subgraph, and Cheah and Corneil (1990) proved that deciding whether such a d-regular subgraph exists is NP-complete for every d ≥ 2 ([as cited by Cornelissen et al., arXiv:1310.2387](https://arxiv.org/abs/1310.2387)). Erickson poses the exactly-twice, edge-at-most-once "double-Hamiltonian circuit" as an exercise, and the CS/ECE 374 solution mentions a 5-vertex, 11-edge gadget for it ([Erickson Ch. 12](https://jeffe.cs.illinois.edu/teaching/algorithms/book/12-nphard.pdf); [CS/ECE 374 HW10](https://courses.grainger.illinois.edu/cs374/sp2017/homework/hw10.pdf)). Ganian et al. treat connected f-factors of simple graphs with growing f ([Ganian et al., Algorithmica 2019](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/143876/1/journal-factor.pdf)). Liu et al. summarise Ganian et al. as an [a,a]-HAM result, which blurs the distinction between simple subgraphs and multigraphs. The README should cite Ganian et al. only for the trail/f-factor setting.

## What the README should cite and claim

The README's title and summary currently present the NP-completeness results as its own. They should instead say that the closed-walk versions are classical and that the README supplies the open-walk versions. A possible title is "Open walks that visit every vertex a bounded number of times: NP-completeness via the classical k-walk reductions". Beyond that, the README needs a short related-work section and five targeted edits. It should attribute the leaf gadget to Jackson–Wormald Theorem 6.1 (j = 1) and the triangle gadget to Liu et al. Lemma 1 and CS/ECE 374. It should credit the Euler lemma to Jackson–Wormald and Bérczi–Mnich–Vincze. It should label the closed-walk and lexicographic remarks as known, with the exact citations below. It should replace K ≥ n−1 with K ≥ Δ(G), and it should point the trail remark at Cheah–Corneil. When citing Jackson–Wormald, use the paper's actual title, "k-walks of graphs"; Liu et al. miscite it as "k-walks in graphs".

A related-work paragraph along these lines would frame the contribution accurately:

> The closed-walk versions of both problems are classical. Broersma and Göbel [BG90] proved that deciding whether a graph has a closed walk visiting every vertex exactly k times is NP-complete for every fixed k. Jackson and Wormald [JW90, Thm 6.1] extended this, and the at-most-k version, to j-connected graphs; for j = 1 their construction attaches k−1 pendant leaves to every vertex. [JW90] also define (exact) k-walks as connected spanning sub-multigraphs of (2k)×G with prescribed degrees, and observe that G has an exact k-walk iff G[K̄_k] is Hamiltonian. Liu, Sheffield and Westover [LSW24, Lemma 1] give the reduction with k−1 pendant triangles, and the k = 2 cases are standard exercises [Erickson Ch. 12; CS/ECE 374; MIT 6.890]. The open s–t form of the multiplicity characterization appears in the many-visits TSP literature [BMV]. We did not find the open-walk (Hamiltonian-path) versions stated. We record them here with proofs that handle walks ending inside a gadget, together with an exhaustive check on small graphs.

Before publishing even that hedged sentence, the authors should do four checks: obtain Broersma–Göbel's full text from *Ars Combinatoria* 29A; look for Pruesse's 1990 thesis; run a "cited by" sweep on both 1990 papers; and search MathOverflow and cs.StackExchange by hand. If Broersma–Göbel's "k-traceable" preprint covers open walks, the README's results become fully published, and the framing should then be "self-contained proofs and verification of known results".

## Conclusion

These results look new mainly because two literatures do not talk to each other. Structural graph theory has studied "k-walks" since 1990, and TSP algorithmics has used the "many-visits" multiplicity representation since 1984. Both rest on the same Euler-multiplicity lemma, and neither cites the other. The README sits at their intersection, and its most useful contribution is as a bridge. It states the open s–t degree pattern from the Many-Visits TSP side alongside the k-walk reductions, and it supplies proofs and code that make the endpoint cases checkable. The exact-versus-leaf obstruction shows that this care is not pedantry. The same gadget subtlety that the README isolates under "Leaves fail here" slipped through Jackson and Wormald's one-line proof of the exact case at j = 1.

If the authors want a result that is new rather than newly phrased, the gaps in the literature point to where to look. The research found no source on open-walk bounded-visit problems in bounded-degree graphs, and none on open directed walks. Liu et al.'s closed-walk dichotomies, such as "[a,b]-HAM is NP-hard iff b < d" at maximum degree d, rely on degree budgets that open walks perturb at exactly two vertices. Whether those thresholds survive for open walks is an unanswered and natural next question. The README's verified deciders are well suited to probe it on small graphs.
